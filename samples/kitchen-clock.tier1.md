The Kitchen Clock
Rain. Half past six, and Daniel Mercer was just locking the front door of his father's repair shop when the sky let go. No warning—one breath dry, the next the pavement had gone dark and every crack between the cobblestones was a tiny, running river of dirty water. Across the street, the bakery was pulling down its shutters for the night. A woman in a yellow coat rushed past with a paper bag clutched to her chest like something precious, and somewhere behind the rooftops a train horn rolled out—low, long, and frankly mournful.
Daniel ducked under the shop's narrow awning and looked up at the clock above the pharmacy. Twenty-three minutes. He'd need every one of them.
Bellwether. He'd lived there for most of his thirty-two years, and Bellwether was one of those towns built entirely in the space between two places that actually mattered. The highway slid past five miles to the south. The river divided the old neighborhood from the new houses—as if the boundary needed to be carved into the landscape to keep things honest. And the railway ran into a city where people went to study, to work, or to start over. Daniel had never once gone to do any of those things. He'd left for university at nineteen, come home after his second year, and spent the decade since then with his hands inside clocks and radios and whatever else strangers hauled through his father's door.
Eleven months. That's how long his father had been dead.
The shop still smelled of machine oil and cedar shavings. Daniel had kept every single thing where it belonged: brass tools on their hooks, jars of screws lined up on the shelves like soldiers, the old radio beside the window that hadn't worked properly since the winter of 2018. Customers would ask, now and then, whether he planned to modernize. He'd say he'd think about it. He never did. (You know how that goes.)
That afternoon, a letter had arrived from a property company in the city. They wanted the building. They wanted to turn it into a coffee shop. The offer was generous—so generous it was almost an insult. Daniel read it three times, folded it with great care, and slipped it beneath the cash register. Then he spent the rest of the afternoon pretending it didn't exist.
Seven o'clock. He was supposed to meet his younger sister, Emma, at the central station. She'd called that morning to say she was coming home for the weekend. Daniel hadn't laid eyes on her since the funeral, when she'd stayed two nights and then gone back to her job in the city.
He walked into the rain.
By the time he reached the station he was soaked, jacket clinging to his shoulders, hair a wet mess against his forehead. The station building looked older than most of the town itself—green-painted benches, a ticket window that hadn't been opened in years, a ceiling stained dark by decades of smoke. A vending machine hummed in the corner. Across the tracks, a man in a red scarf read his newspaper under a fluorescent light that flickered like it was coming to the end of its patience.
Emma sat on a bench, a small suitcase beside her. Same black boots she'd worn to the funeral. Same gray sweater Daniel remembered from their childhood. She smiled when she saw him—but it took a beat for that smile to reach her eyes.
"You're late," she said.
"The weather."
"You've always had an excuse."
He laughed, assuming she was joking. Then he noticed she wasn't. Not a joke at all.
They walked to a café two streets away, one of the vanishingly few places in town that stayed open past eight. The owner recognized them on sight and brought two cups of tea without a single question. For a while they talked about the safe stuff: Emma's apartment, the new manager at her office, the neighbor who'd gone and painted his house an alarming shade of blue. Daniel told her how Mrs. Holloway had finally carried in the clock she'd been promising to have fixed for six years.
Neither of them said a word about the shop.
Emma broke that. She set her cup down on the saucer and looked at him through the steam.
"Are you going to sell it?"
Daniel glanced at the window. The rain had settled into a steady drizzle, and the streetlights had started bleeding into the wet glass.
"I haven't decided."
"You have decided. You're just waiting for someone else to tell you it's okay."
He wanted to argue. He wanted to lay it all out—that he'd stayed when she left, that he'd spent months buried in their father's paperwork and cooling his heels in hospital waiting rooms, that somebody had to keep the shop running. But the words sounded small before he even said them, and he was exhausted from dragging around arguments that had nowhere to go.
"What would you do?" he asked.
Emma looked at her hands. "I don't know."
It was the first honest thing either of them had said all evening.
Their father had been a quiet man—the kind who showed love through doing rather than saying. He sharpened Daniel's tools without ever being asked. He left a thermos of coffee beside Emma's textbooks when she studied late at the kitchen table. When their mother died, he repaired the kitchen clock that had stopped on the very afternoon she passed away. He worked on it for three nights, refusing to explain why it mattered. When he finished, he set it on the mantelpiece and never touched it again.
After the funeral, Daniel found a notebook in his father's desk. Mostly it was practical stuff: measurements, lists of parts, diagrams of mechanisms. But on the last page, in handwriting that had gone unsteady with age, was a single sentence: Things are worth keeping when someone still knows what they're for.
Daniel had read it so many times that he could no longer say whether it was advice, or just something his father scribbled to himself.
"Do you remember the clock in the kitchen?" Emma asked.
"Of course."
"He fixed it because Mom liked hearing it at night. The ticking helped her sleep."
Daniel stared at her. He'd never known that. Not once.
"She told me once," Emma said. "I was little. I couldn't sleep either, and she said the clock made the house feel less empty."
Silence. Outside, a bus thundered through a puddle and threw water against the curb.
"I thought you hated this town," Daniel said.
"I did, sometimes. I thought leaving meant I was going somewhere better." She paused. "But I think I mostly wanted to find out who I was when I wasn't somebody's daughter or somebody's little sister."
"Did you find out?"
Emma smiled, faint. "Some days."
They stayed until the owner started stacking chairs, and by then the rain had stopped. They walked back to the station the long way, through the square. The town hall clock struck nine, and the sound carried across the empty streets like something being set down gently.
Emma would leave again on Sunday. She hadn't said whether she planned to visit more often, and Daniel didn't ask. For the first time that evening, he understood that he didn't need an answer to every question before taking the next step.
At the station, Emma picked up her suitcase.
"Come visit me," she said. "Not because you have errands in the city. Just come."
"I could do that."
"You could," she said. "But will you?"
He looked at the platform, at the tracks vanishing into the dark past the station lights. Twelve minutes until the next train. Strange thing, how a timetable made a place feel temporary—as if everything were just waiting for permission to leave.
"I'll try," he said.
Emma hugged him. It was an awkward embrace, the kind shared by people who'd spent too long pretending to be fine. When she pulled back, her eyes were wet. Neither of them mentioned it.
"Take care of yourself, Danny."
"You too, Em."
The train arrived and she boarded. Daniel watched through the window as she found a seat, put her suitcase overhead, and pulled out her phone. The train began to move. Its lights swept past the signal box, crossed the bridge, and folded around the bend.
He stayed on the platform one minute longer.
Then he walked back to the shop.
The streets were nearly empty. A few stray drops slipped off the awnings, and the wind carried the smell of wet leaves from the river. Daniel unlocked the front door and switched on the light above the workbench. The room looked exactly as it had that morning—but for once he actually saw it. The worn patch on the stool where his father used to sit. The pencil marks along the edge of the bench. The dent in the wall where a clock had fallen during a summer storm.
He took the property company's letter out from under the register and read it one more time. The offer was still generous. The building needed repairs. The roof would need replacing eventually. And the business had barely made enough money to cover expenses over the previous winter. Selling would be the logical thing. The practical reasons were all still there—none of them had vanished just because he'd spent one evening talking to his sister.
Daniel folded the letter and put it in his desk.
He didn't decide to keep the shop forever. He didn't suddenly understand his father's life, or his own. He just picked up the broken radio from beside the window, carried it to the workbench, removed the back panel, laid out his tools, and started looking at the wiring.
The repair would take time. He might not succeed.
For now, that was enough.
At ten minutes past ten, the kitchen clock at the back of the shop began to tick. Daniel had wound it that morning, as he did every day, and he'd barely heard it before. But now its steady rhythm moved through the quiet room, marking one second and then the next.
He sat with the radio open in front of him and listened.
Outside, a late train passed through Bellwether without stopping.
